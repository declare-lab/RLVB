Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs

Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs

RLVB teaches them to act on what they believe

Richmond Sin Jing Xuan1* Peilin Feng1* Wenbin Wang2 Soujanya Poria1

1DeCLaRe Lab, Nanyang Technological University  ·  2Independent  ·  *Equal contribution

Paper ↗ Code ↗ Models ↗ Episodes

Agents on long tasks often stop too soon. They commit to an answer while their own stated belief still names several possible answers. During training, the simulator can compute the exact posterior over the hidden answer after every step. RLVB takes this posterior as the belief supported by the evidence, and rewards the agent for taking further steps while several answers remain and for committing once the evidence is sufficient.

The problem

We ask each model to write a belief line before every action, such as Possible: cups 2, 4. The base models often write that several answers remain and still commit in the same turn.

On turns with insufficient evidence where the belief line still names several answers, Base (w/ belief) commits in 3 to 68% of cases. After RLVB, the same models commit in 0.1 to 2.2% of cases.

An outcome reward does not fix this, because it pays a lucky guess as much as an answer backed by evidence. GRPO and PPO with the outcome reward lose 1.0 and 5.1 points on average. For the base models, a check in the harness that rejects the first answer and asks the agent to look at its evidence again changes accuracy by at most 1.9 points on HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds, because the harness cannot read the observations.

The method

RL with verifiable rewards checks the final answer. The simulator knows more than that, since it knows the rules linking the hidden answer to the evidence. It can therefore verify the belief supported by the evidence at every step, and this belief shows whether the agent should take a further step or answer.

  1. The turn. The agent writes its reasoning, then a belief line with the answers it still sees as possible, and an action line that takes a further step or gives the answer.
  2. The teacher. The simulator updates the exact posterior after each observation. A teacher looks ahead on this posterior and chooses the next action.
  3. The reward. The reward compares the agent's action with the teacher's action. It does not read the belief line, and a guess receives a low reward.
  4. The update. At each history the agent samples several turns, and the turns with a higher reward become more likely. The update trains the whole turn, including the belief line.
  5. At test time. The trained agent acts alone, from its history of actions and observations. It needs no simulator, posterior or teacher. It also runs on RoboQuest, where the possible states cannot be listed, the belief is open-ended free text, and no exact posterior exists.

Results

We train three vision-language models on HiddenGames and test them on unseen games of HiddenGames and on Pro-Worlds and RoboQuest, which are never seen in training. RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by up to 15.1 points on Pro-Worlds and up to 32.8 points on RoboQuest. In the charts, PPO (belief + action) is PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge, RLVB (EM) uses expectation maximization as the learner, and RLVB without the belief line outputs only an action.

RLVB also changes how the episodes end. It gives more right answers with enough evidence and far fewer wrong answers that come too early, and it takes more evidence-gathering steps than every baseline.

Why it works

To find where the gain comes from, we replay every test episode and group the answers by the evidence the agent has when it answers. For every method, 66 to 92% of the answers given with sufficient evidence are correct. RLVB reaches this level of evidence far more often than the base model.

The gain over the base model splits into a part from answering at higher levels of evidence and a part from accuracy at the same level of evidence. Almost all of the gain comes from the first part.

Training does not make the belief line consistently more exact. Over all turns it is exact less often on Pro-Worlds, by 4.9 to 7.8 points, and more often on RoboQuest for two of the three models. The steps are also not more informative, since about 21% of the actions of both agents add evidence on RoboQuest at the same state. When either agent commits after a single-answer line, it gives the answer named by the line in at least 97% of cases.

Training mainly links the decision to commit to the agent's own belief. Because the trained agent acts on its belief, we expect a better reading of the evidence to translate into better answers.

Benchmarks

In every game the answer is hidden. The agent must take actions to collect evidence and decide when it has seen enough to answer. Pro-Worlds is a new benchmark in which the agent views a large world through a small window and moves the window to gather visual evidence.
